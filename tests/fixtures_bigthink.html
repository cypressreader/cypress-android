It was nearly a full century ago that enough data came in to map out both the distance to a significant number of extragalactic objects and also measure the amount that their light was redshifted: interpreted as an apparent recession speed away from us. When the distance and recession velocity of those objects were plotted against one another, an intriguing relationship emerged: the farther away we looked, the faster an object seemed to be moving away from us. Twice as far meant twice as fast; ten times as far meant ten times as fast. This linear relationship became known as Hubble’s Law (or more recently, the Hubble-Lemaître Law, since Georges Lemaître derived it first), with the slope of that line known as the Hubble constant.

However, it turns out that this is a complete misnomer: not only does the expansion rate of the Universe change with time, but it isn’t even constant across all of space right at this very moment. In other words, there’s nothing constant about the Hubble “constant” at all! The variation of the expansion rate with time was predicted from the star, but the variation in space came as a surprise: only with the discovery of dark energy did we realize this was the case. And yet, dark energy may turn out to be the Hubble constant’s ultimate savior, as if it truly is a cosmological constant, then one day, the Hubble parameter will, far into the future, actually become a constant after all. Here’s the science of what’s really going on.

Observationally, the story is seemingly straightforward. Beginning in the 1920s, our telescope technology became advanced enough — with large-enough aperture telescopes and the development of photographic plates — that we could start to resolve and identify individual stars in objects (i.e., galaxies) that were outside of our own Milky Way. The first such star was identified by Edwin Hubble in 1923: a Cepheid variable star, or a special class of star where, if you could measure the amount of time it took

  • to brighten to its maximum,
  • fainten to a minimum,
  • and re-brighten back to its maximum again,

you could know how intrinsically bright it was.

Then, you could compare the observed brightness of that star (which you measured) with the intrinsic brightness that you just figured out, and that allows you to figure out the distance to it: the same way that, if you had a light bulb or candle with a known intrinsic brightness, then seeing how bright it appears would allow you to figure out how far away it is. This method for figuring out a distance in astronomy — the standard candle method — has since been extended to far more classes of objects than just Cepheid variable stars, and is one of the most useful tools in an observational astronomer’s arsenal.

Then, in addition to figuring out the distance to that object, you can measure the light coming from that object as well. Rather than simply accumulating all the light of all different wavelengths together (a bolometric measurement), or even by measuring the light across a variety of differently colored filters (a photometric measurement), you could break that light up into all of its different component wavelengths, and measure the amount of light emitted at each specific wavelength along the way: a spectroscopic measurement. This technique, of spectroscopy, was the other key ingredient to uncovering the expanding Universe, and in turn, Hubble’s Law.

When you break light up into its component wavelengths, you get to see an enormous number of features about it, including lines of two varieties:

  • dark lines, known as absorption features, that occur at specific wavelengths and with specific intensities depending on the amount and density of any species of molecule, atom, or ion present,
  • and bright lines, known as emission features, that occur at those same specific wavelengths that emerge from electrons transitioning between an excited state and a lower-energy state in molecules, atoms, or ions, at those same specific wavelengths.

By comparing the observed wavelengths of (usually) absorption or (sometimes) emission features, we can determine an object’s redshift, and calculate the equivalent recession speed that corresponds to that redshift.

Then, by plotting distance and redshift for the same sets of objects, together, we can make a graph for all sorts of extragalactic objects. This was done first by Georges Lemaître in an obscure 1927 paper (using much of Edwin Hubble’s data), then by Howard Robertson, independently, in 1928, and finally — and much more comprehensively — by Edwin Hubble himself in 1929. Although the points didn’t all lie exactly on a straight line, the overall trend was clear: the farther away an object was from us, on average, the greater its observed redshift, and hence, the greater its inferred recession velocity was.

In all the time since those early measurements from the 1920s, throughout the early, mid, and late 20th century and even into the 1990s, we extended those measurements of distance and redshift to larger numbers of objects and evermore distant objects. Still, the slope of that best-fit line remained constant, and was named the Hubble constant. (In fact, the famed Hubble Space Telescope got its name because its primary mission was to pin down the measurement of that constant: the expansion rate of the Universe today.)

Starting in the late 1990s, however, astronomers and astrophysicists made a shocking discovery. At the greatest of distances, the observed redshift-distance relation no longer followed a straight line, but instead departed from it. The theoretical implications of this — interpreted as evidence for the existence of dark energy — were astounding.
