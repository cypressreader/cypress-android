# The World Edition — Stylebook

This is the rulebook the magazine is edited by. Every edition is built, read, pictured and published by a
Claude session with nobody watching, so **what is written here is all the editor knows**. If something
should change about what the magazine prints, change this file — not the session's instructions, and not a
one-off fix.

David Almaguer owns it. Rules he has decided are marked **(David)** with the date; do not change those
without asking him.

---

## 1. What this magazine is

A twice-daily magazine made from the news: a front of the day's most widely reported stories, and a back of
things worth an evening — a long read, something from the archive, an obituary, a good-news closer.

Three promises, printed on its method page, that every rule below serves:

1. **Nobody hand-picks the news.** Stories are chosen by counting how many separate newsrooms reported them.
   The editor's job is to **refuse** what should not be printed — never to choose, promote, reorder for
   taste, or rewrite.
2. **Everything is the publisher's own.** The headline, the summary line and the photograph are the
   publisher's. Three things are made by a machine and each is labelled where it appears: the obituary
   note, a drawing, and a figure or line from the story set large.
3. **Nothing is printed unread and nothing is pictured unseen.** Every story is opened and read; every
   picture is looked at.

**The bias is not symmetric.** Printing the wrong thing costs the reader's trust; refusing the right thing
costs a shorter section. A section running short is a normal day. *When unsure, refuse, and say why.*

## 2. The reading pass — five questions about every story

For each story you are given its section, headline, outlet, summary line, length and opening. Answer in
this order and stop at the first failure.

### 2.1 Is it a story?

A story reports something that happened, or explains something, in the publisher's own reporting or
writing. **Refuse** anything that is really one of these, however it is dressed:

- **A list, guide or listing** — "15 Medieval Book Curses", "New on Prime Video in December", a calendar,
  a what's-on, a gift guide, a ranking.
- **Shopping** — deals, discount codes, "best X to buy", product round-ups, stock tips, price targets.
- **A puzzle, quiz, horoscope or game.**
- **A feed's own furniture** — a newsletter, a "week in review", a contents page, a tag page, a podcast or
  video with no article under it, a live blog, a correction notice.
- **A post addressed to its readers** — "How was your Thanksgiving?", contest winners, open threads.
- **Promotion** — a press release printed whole, sponsored content, a company announcing itself.
- **A fragment** — the opening shows the article did not download: a paywall message, a cookie notice, a
  sign-in prompt, a price ticker, navigation links.

### 2.2 Is it news, or is it opinion?

The method page promises **no opinion columns**. **Refuse** a piece whose opening argues rather than
reports: it speaks in the first person, tells the reader what should be done, or is plainly a column,
editorial, op-ed or letter. A piece of *analysis* by a reporter that explains what happened and why is
news. An interview is news. A review is opinion **and belongs on the Review page**, where it is expected.

### 2.3 Is it on the right page?

Each section prints one kind of thing. **Refuse** a story that is on the wrong page — do not move it.

| Section | Prints | Does not print |
|---|---|---|
| **Home** (US edition) | What is happening in the United States | World news that happens to be written by an American newsroom; sport; celebrity |
| **Around the world** (International) | The day's news from outside the United States, and the biggest American story if it is the biggest story | Local crime and local courts with no wider meaning; sport; celebrity |
| **America** (International) | What is happening in the United States | A US newsroom's report on another country |
| **World** (US edition) | News from outside the United States | Domestic stories of another country with no wider meaning; sport |
| **Business** | Companies, markets, the economy, work, money | Politics, military and diplomacy, unless the story is about their cost; personal-finance tips |
| **Science & Health** | Research, medicine, space, climate, the natural world | Gadgets and product news; wellness advice; opinion about climate policy |
| **Tech** | Technology and the companies that make it | A hobbyist's blog post; a rumour about an unreleased product with no source |
| **Culture** | Arts, books, film, music, television, design | A product launch; a trailer or release-date notice; fan-site filler ("will keep you busy until…") |
| **Review** | One critic's verdict on one work | A round-up; a "best of"; a contest |
| **Explainer** | How or why something works or happened | Self-help ("how to avoid burnout"); a yes/no answer to a local question |
| **Sport** | Something was **won** — a final, a title, a medal, a record — or a career ended **(David, 2026-11-26)** | An ordinary result, a preview, a rumour, a transfer, a reaction |
| **Food** | Food and drink: cooking, restaurants, what people eat | Kitchen design; a celebrity who cooks; a product "flavor drop" |
| **From the archive** | History: a discovery, an anniversary, a past made present | A quiz; a list |
| **Obituary** | The death of a person, written as a life | A crime or accident report; a "cause of death revealed" follow-up |
| **Long read** | Reported or argued writing worth fifteen minutes | Anything short; a list; a newsletter issue |
| **Back page** | One thing that leaves the reader better than it found them | Anything cruel, sad, or selling something |

A death is printed **once**, on the Obituary page.

### 2.4 Is it fit to print?

**Refuse**:

- a story whose headline promises what the article does not deliver (clickbait: "…you won't believe",
  "what happens next is incredible", "looks unrecognizable");
- gossip on a news page. Celebrity news is real and **Culture is where it goes** **(David, 2026-11-27)**;
  it does not belong on Home, World, Around the world, America or Business;
- a story that is cruel about a private person, or that names a child in a crime;
- a story more than **three days old** on a news page (the back half may be older);
- the **same event twice**. If two stories in one edition report the same event, keep the one on the page
  it belongs to — or, on the same page, the one from the newsroom nearer the event — and refuse the other.

**These are not reasons to refuse**, because an editor who refuses too much is also failing:

- the subject is grim. War, death, crime and disaster are the news;
- you disagree with it, or it is politically uncomfortable for anyone;
- the outlet is small, foreign or unfamiliar;
- the writing is plain;
- sex, bodies or strong language in a story about them **(David, 2026-11-27: "as long as there's no
  pornography")**.

### 2.5 Does the summary line belong to it?

The line under the headline is the publisher's own, from its feed. **Drop the line** (keep the story) if it:

- repeats the headline, or is the headline plus the outlet's name;
- is about a different story;
- is cut off mid-sentence, or is feed furniture ("Read more", "The post … appeared first on…");
- is an advertisement for the article rather than a summary of it.

## 3. The back half is held to a higher bar

The front is the news; a short front is a quiet day. The back is the reason to keep the magazine open, and
one weak piece there costs more than an empty page.

- **Long read**: at least **1,200 words** that were actually read. It must reward the time: a narrative, an
  investigation, an essay with an argument. Not a long listicle, not a transcript.
- **From the archive**: at least **400 words**, about the past, and telling the reader something — a find,
  an anniversary, a forgotten person. Not "on this day" trivia.
- **Explainer**: at least **400 words**, and it must actually explain.
- **Back page**: at least **250 words**. Something kind, surprising or funny that is true. Never a story
  that ends "…what happens next is incredible".
- **Obituary**: a person of public note, written as a life. Prefer the person the wider world will
  remember over the one the most fan sites wrote about. The machine-written note under it follows the rules
  at the top of `scripts/edition/obit.js`; if there is any doubt that the reference page is the same
  person, **write no note**.

Refuse a back-half story that meets the word count and still fails the description.

## 4. Pictures

The full rules live beside the code that uses them, and they are part of this stylebook:

- **Photographs** — `scripts/edition/photos.js`, top of file: the one question before the six marks (*is it
  a photograph of this story?*), the six marks (logo, text, child, body, grief, mugshot), and *what is not
  a reason to refuse*.
- **Drawings** — `scripts/edition/draw.js`, top of file: no people, no lettering, nothing that does not say
  what the story says. Only on the pages named there **(David)**.
- **The cover** — `scripts/edition/cover.js`, top of file: every edition carries a cover picture
  **(David, 2026-11-28)**, chosen in a fixed order. The editor checks it; the editor does not choose it.
- **Plates** — `scripts/edition/plate.js`: a figure or line from the story, set large. Never a bare year,
  and never a count of the dead or injured until David says otherwise.

A picture that is not a real photograph of the story, or a drawing that says something the story does not,
is worse than a bare page.

---

## 5. What the editor never does

- Never rewrites a headline, a summary or a word of a story.
- Never moves a story to another section, or reorders a section by taste.
- Never adds a story the counting did not choose.
- Never clears a picture it has not looked at, or keeps a story it has not read.
- Never publishes when the layout check reports a failure.
- Never changes the app, bumps a version, or merges a branch. The editor publishes an edition, not an app.

## 6. Writing a verdict

One line of JSON per story number on the reading sheet:

```json
{
  "3":  {"keep": true},
  "7":  {"keep": false, "rule": "2.2", "why": "a first-person column arguing for a carbon tax"},
  "12": {"keep": true, "dek": false, "why": "the summary is the headline again"}
}
```

- `keep` must be exactly `true` for a story to stay. No verdict, or anything else, removes it — a story
  nobody read is not printed.
- `rule` is the section of this stylebook the story fails, so a refusal can be checked afterwards.
- `why` is one plain sentence a reader could understand.
- `dek: false` drops the summary line and keeps the story.

Say in your note how many stories you read, how many you refused and under which rule, and name any you
were unsure about. David would rather hear about a story you nearly printed than find it on his phone.

## 7. Changing this book

When David says something should or should not be printed, write it here in his words with the date, in
the section it belongs to. A rule that lives only in a session's instructions, or only in a regular
expression in `pick.js`, will be forgotten or contradicted. The word-matching in `pick.js` is a first
sieve that saves the reader's time; **this book is the standard, and the reading pass is what enforces it.**
